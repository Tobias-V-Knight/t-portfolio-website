# Project 1: 4mativ GPS fleet analytics

Live case, Carlson Analytics Lab, spring 2026. In `projects` today as
`4mativ-anomaly-detection`, `content.ts` line 248, with every prose field a
placeholder.

**Note for whoever writes the copy.** The slug and title say anomaly detection.
Anomaly detection is one of four layers, and M1 line 33 frames the project as
something else entirely. That framing is better and it is quoted below.

## The business question

4mativ coordinates school transport across two schools using GPS from sixteen
vendors, each with its own hardware and its own polling standard, so the raw
feeds are inconsistent and not comparable (M1 line 33).

The operational problem, in M1's words at line 33: raw GPS blends hardware
failures with driver behaviour, so when a route underperforms nobody can tell
whether it is a device problem or a driver problem.

The key question, M1 line 35: how can GPS data quality and route execution be
scored **independently** of each other, to drive vendor accountability.

That is the whole design in one sentence, and it is why the pipeline has the
shape it has. Separating the two axes is the idea. Everything downstream, the
four layers, the three lenses, the quadrant matrix, exists to keep a device
failure from being read as a driver failure.

Who asked: 4mativ, as the live case client. What hung on the answer, M1 section
4: which vendors get a performance warning, which get a hardware audit, and
which get held up as the benchmark. The interventions are written per quadrant
at M1 lines 307 to 312.

## The data

From M1 lines 47 to 54 and Appendix C.

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Trips | 14,859 |
| Routes | 640, of which 161 had at least three trips and entered the triage analysis |
| Vendors | 16, of which 3 were excluded from the leaderboard for having three trips or fewer |
| Schools | 2 |
| Period | September 2025 to February 2026 |
| GPS pings | About 2.35 million |

Twelve raw CSVs, listed with sizes at M1 Appendix C and M4. The big ones are
`trip_positions.csv` at 170 MB of raw pings and `trips.csv` at 24 MB of trip
metadata. `Data/` is gitignored as client owned and privacy sensitive (M4).

The one data fact that shaped the method, M1 lines 160 to 169: polling interval
varies by GPS provider. Two providers poll every 60 seconds, one every 30, two
every 20. At 30 mph that is 804 m between pings on the slowest and 268 m on the
fastest (M3). A 60 second vendor therefore looks worse than a 20 second vendor
on every raw signal metric while being no worse at all, which is why Layer 1
scores each trip only against other trips from the same provider.

There are also two stop ID systems, M1 lines 190 to 193: `route_stop_id` is a
stop instance on one route, `stop_location_id` is a physical place shared across
routes. Miss that and a bus on the wrong route earns credit at a shared stop.

## The method

Four layers. M1 section 3 is the authority; M6 is the source for Layer 3.

**Layer 1, GPS connection health.** One Isolation Forest per GPS provider group,
over four engineered features per trip: average pings per minute, the largest
continuous silence, the fraction of pings with identical consecutive
coordinates, and actual over expected ping count for that vendor's interval.
Scores normalise to 0 to 100 **within provider**, which is the fairness fix
above. Trips with no pings score zero. Runtime about 15 minutes.

**Layer 2, event triggering.** Answers when the bus was actually in service.
Haversine geofencing finds the first ping within 100 m of the first planned stop
and the last within 100 m of the last, giving an active window, with five
fallback methods for the edge cases. Within that window, a stop counts as
visited if any ping fell within 80 m of it, recorded with dwell time and
confidence. Pings within 15 minutes either side of the first and last stop are
stripped as vehicle staging. Runtime about 20 minutes, reading 2.35 million rows
in chunks.

**Layer 3, anomaly detection.** DBSCAN at eps 150 m over historical pings
grouped by route and trip type learns each route's normal corridor. Morning and
afternoon runs train separately so opposite direction corridors do not
contaminate each other, which is a nice catch. A route needs at least three
trips to get its own model, otherwise a global model takes over. Every ping then
gets one of five labels. On top of that, Local Outlier Factor at the trip level
over eight features, flagging trips scoring above 3.0. Runtime about 35 minutes,
the slow layer.

**Layer 4, composite scoring.** Rolls trip level components up to route and
vendor and applies three weighting schemes. Runtime about 5 minutes.

The anomaly taxonomy, M1 Appendix A, is worth having in full because it is the
part a non technical reader understands immediately:

| Flag | Meaning | How it is detected | Who to call |
|---|---|---|---|
| DETOUR | Bus left the expected corridor | Ping more than 150 m off the learned corridor at sustained low or medium speed | Vendor operations |
| WRONG_ROUTE | Bus is on a different route | Corridor mismatch plus stop visit failure below 15% | Vendor operations |
| GPS_JUMP | Physically impossible move | Implied speed above 80 mph between consecutive pings | The vendor's GPS provider |
| FROZEN_DEVICE | Repeated identical coordinates | Near zero variance across consecutive pings | The vendor's GPS provider |
| DATA_GAP | Signal lost over five minutes | Gap above 300 seconds between pings | Driver training or cellular coverage |
| noise | Unclassifiable ping | DBSCAN label of minus one | Nobody. It is a mathematical byproduct, not a failure |

M1 lines 507 to 509 sort those into behavioural, hardware and coverage errors
and route each to a different phone call. That mapping, method to corrective
action, is the deliverable a client can act on and it is the strongest thing in
the document.

**The three lenses.** The scoring design, M1 Appendix B. Five components, all
0 to 1 at trip level: completion, corridor, coverage, data quality and on time.
Three stakeholders weight them differently:

| Component | Parent facing | Vendor scorecard | Real time dispatch |
|---|---|---|---|
| completion | 70% | 45% | 25% |
| corridor | 20% | 30% | 35% |
| coverage | | 15% | 25% |
| data quality | 10% | 8% | 15% |
| on time | | 2% | |

On time sits at 2%, vendor lens only, because the timing was not validated. M1
section 7 is honest about why: nobody had confirmed whether `pickup_time` is the
scheduled arrival at the first student stop or the departure from the depot, and
per stop scheduled times were not in the export. Shipping it at 2% with the
reason written down, rather than shipping it at full weight or dropping it, is a
good judgement call and it is a case study beat.

Route execution score, used as the Y axis of every matrix: completion times 0.60
plus corridor times 0.40.

## The findings

**The methodology finding is the headline.** GPS devices start pinging when the
engine starts, long before the bus reaches its first stop. Those depot pings
register against stops that happen to sit near the depot, and they inflate the
completion rate. Strip them with the active window and the fleet completion
figure **falls by 7 points** (M1 lines 101 to 107, M2 line 18). The team kept the
uncorrected figure in `docs/reference/layer2_combined.ipynb` for the comparison
slide and told the client in writing not to use it as an operational benchmark.

A team that found its own headline number was wrong, corrected it downward,
showed the client both, and explained the physics: that is the story. It does
not need the absolute levels to land, which is convenient, because they are
withheld.

**Numbers withheld.** Both completion levels, the fleet connection scores with
and without no signal trips, and every per vendor figure. All in M1 section 4.

**A discrepancy T should know about.** M3, dated 2026-04-27, states the drop as
16 points. M1, dated two days later, and M2, state it as 7 points. M2 line 18 is
explicit that the smaller figure is the one presented in the final deck. So M1
wins and M3 is stale, but two handoff documents in the same delivered folder
disagree about the project's headline number, and if T reuses M3 he will quote
the wrong one.

**Coverage, not signal quality, is the problem.** When devices are connected the
fleet scores well. Including no signal trips drags the average down hard. The
hardware works; too many trips produce no GPS at all (M1 lines 279 to 285). Two
vendors have so many no signal trips that their real device quality is masked.

**Completion is bimodal, and that is the operationally urgent finding.** Several
high volume vendors show a dumbbell distribution: trips complete either none of
their stops or all of them, with very little in between (M1 lines 286 to 292).
Drivers are not skipping a stop or two. They are either running the route or not
running it. An average completion rate hides that completely, and it changes the
intervention from "tighten up" to "find out why whole routes are not being run".

**Anomalies are navigational, not hardware.** Detour dominates the mix. Drivers
are leaving planned corridors rather than devices malfunctioning (M1 lines 314
to 318). With a caveat the team raised themselves: widespread off corridor
detections across many vendors may mean the official route definitions are out
of date rather than that the drivers are wrong, so M1 section 7 tells the client
to verify `route_stops.csv` before using detour rates in a contract review.
Flagging the thing that would invalidate your own headline is the second good
judgement beat in this project.

**The vendor matrix.** The two axes stay separate right to the end, which is the
payoff of the whole design (M1 lines 294 to 312):

| Quadrant | GPS | Execution | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operational Excellence | High | High | The benchmark. Document what they do and spread it |
| Unconfident Success | Low | High | Drivers are executing and operations cannot see it. Audit the hardware |
| Behavioral Risk | High | Low | Fully visible, and visibly deviating. Retrain |
| Critical Failure | Low | Low | Visibility and execution both broken. Highest priority |

Unconfident Success is the quadrant that only exists because quality and
execution were scored independently. A single blended score would have buried
those vendors in the middle of a leaderboard and sent them a driver warning
instead of a hardware audit.

# Project 2: GED Testing Service candidate segmentation

Live case, Carlson Analytics Lab, MSBA 6411, March 2026. **Not in `projects`.**

## The business question

GED Testing Service serves adults working toward a high school equivalency
credential. The client's framing, from the kickoff and reproduced at G2 slide 2:
most people who create an account never credential, testing resources are spread
unevenly between urban and rural areas, and GEDTS reacts to anecdotal feedback
instead of acting by region or segment. Confidence is a named barrier: a large
share of candidates say they are worried they will not pass, or that they want
to study more, **even when their scores say they are ready**.

The key question the team was set, G2 slide 2:

> Can we identify patterns of testing performance disparity that correlate to
> regional resources and candidate engagement, and what are the potential
> drivers?

What hung on the answer, G2 slides 12 to 14: where GEDTS puts money next, which
of five geographies gets a pilot, and whether the fix is a messaging campaign or
a structural one. The final recommendation says those are different problems in
different places and sequences them accordingly.

**The analytical problem underneath it**, and this is the interesting part.
G1 says it plainly: GEDTS wanted to know which candidates are most engaged, and
**there is no engagement variable in the data**. All that exists is which prep
methods each candidate ticked and whether they eventually credentialed. So the
whole analysis is built around one question: can prep method choice stand in for
engagement well enough to segment on.

That is the case study. Not the clustering. Building a measurable proxy for an
unmeasured construct, from a binary survey, and then earning the right to use it.

**Funnel and population figures from the client kickoff: withheld.** G2 slide 2.

## The data

From G2 appendix B, G1 and G4.

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Candidates in the clustering | 304,137 |
| Rows in the geocoded file | 509,718 |
| Accounts created | 2020-01-01 to 2022-09-30 |
| Scope | United States. Texas excluded per GEDTS scope |
| Prep methods per candidate | 11 binary flags |

Shape: one row per candidate. Eleven binary prep method flags, age at exam,

Exclusions, all declared in G2 appendix A rather than buried: candidates whose

**The geocoding was its own piece of work**, and it is the one piece the

Two findings came straight out of that step. Almost every US candidate sits the
exam in the state they live in, which retired the idea that cross state travel
explained the geographic spread. And every candidate ended up with either full
geography or an explicit international flag, which is what made the county level
layer possible at all.

**State and county level candidate counts and rates: withheld.** G4.

## The method

Five steps, G2 slide 4 and G1 part 1. Each one exists because the previous one
was not enough, which is the right way to present it.

**Step 1, phi coefficient.** Before anything sophisticated: does using each
method relate to credentialing at all. Both variables are binary, so phi, which
is Pearson on binary data. Output is a ranking. What it establishes is that the
eleven methods are not equally informative. What it cannot do is handle the fact
that candidates use several methods at once, which is why there is a step 2.

**Step 2, logistic regression.** The central step. Credentialed or not as the
outcome, all eleven prep methods as simultaneous predictors, output an odds
ratio per method controlling for the others. G1: a candidate using GED Ready and
Adult Ed and online video at once gets each method's contribution separated out.
Ten of eleven methods were retained; the mobile app flag was dropped as not
significant.

| Prep method | Odds ratio | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| GED Ready | 1.34 | 34% higher odds |
| Online course or video | 1.22 | 22% higher odds |
| Books or print | 1.12 | 12% higher odds |
| Adult Ed class | 0.83 | 17% lower odds |
| TV study programme | 0.70 | 30% lower odds |
| Mobile app | About 1.00 | No effect, dropped |

These are odds ratios on candidate study behaviour, not GEDTS performance
figures, which is why they are in this file. The two negative ones are the
finding: engagement **quality** beats engagement **quantity**, and using more
methods is not the same as using better ones.

**Step 3, weighted prep score.** This is the bridge and the idea. Take the log
odds beta behind each odds ratio and use it as a weight:

```
WEIGHTED_PREP_SCORE = sum over methods of ( beta x did this candidate use it )
```

One continuous engagement proxy per candidate. Not a count of methods. Methods
associated with credentialing add, methods associated with not credentialing
subtract. G1 is careful to say why this is principled rather than arbitrary: the
weights are learned from the outcome, not assigned by opinion.

**Step 4, K means, k equals 4.** Features: age at exam, the weighted prep score,
the ten retained binary method flags, gender, race, and reason for taking the
GED. Numeric features standardised, gender one hot encoded, binaries passed
through (G6 cell 5). Tested k from 2 to 7 on a 30,000 row sample, using an elbow
plot and silhouette score, and picked 4 for the balance of separation and
interpretability, then fitted on all 304,137 rows. The elbow and silhouette plot
is at `final materials + data/current/figures/elbow_silhouette.png`.

**Step 5, geographic layer.** Cluster composition and credential rates rolled up
to state and county (G7, `mar_21_geo_analysis.py`). A county needs at least 30
candidates in a cluster to appear in any ranking, which G2 appendix A explains
as a reliability floor for a rate estimate. Counties are ranked by their gap
against their own cluster's national average rather than by absolute rate, so
what surfaces is a county underperforming what its candidate mix predicts, not
just a county with hard candidates. That is the correct choice and it is worth
saying out loud in the case study.

## The findings

**Four segments.** Names, sizes and mean ages, from G1 part 2 and G2 slide 6.

| Segment | Share | Mean age | The one line |
|---|---|---|---|
| GED Ready Completers | 35% | 22.3 | Almost all of them use GED Ready. Highest engagement score. The benchmark behaviour |
| Digital Self Starters | 28% | 22.6 | Demographically near identical to the first group, but almost none use GED Ready. They self study on video and print |
| Institutionally Dependent | 20% | 25.3 | Nearly all attend an Adult Ed class. Essentially none use GED Ready. **The only segment with a negative engagement score** |
| Returning Adults | 18% | 44.1 | Twenty years older than every other segment. Near gender balanced, the only one without a male skew. A quarter are motivated by work |

**The spread between the best and worst segment is 19 points, and demographics
do not explain it** (G2 slide 6). Age, race and gender alone do not produce that
gap. What produces it is what candidates use to prepare and where they live.
That is the finding the whole method was built to reach, and it is stated as a
relative gap in the source, so it survives the publishing line intact.

**Absolute credential rates per segment: withheld.** G1 part 2.

The four segments each fail for a different reason, and that is what makes them
useful rather than decorative:

* **Digital Self Starters are the cheapest win in the data.** They are already
  doing the right behaviour, self directed digital study, with a less effective
  tool. Same age, same motivation, same digital comfort as the top segment. The
  gap closes on a platform switch, not a behaviour change. G1 and G2 slide 8.
* **Institutionally Dependent candidates are showing up and not credentialing.**
  Over nine in ten attend an Adult Ed class. They are not disengaged. But
  classroom instruction alone underperforms self directed digital prep, and the
  likely barriers named at G2 slide 9 are device access, internet access and
  digital literacy, not motivation. Being the only segment with a negative
  engagement score while having the highest class attendance is the sharpest
  single result in the deck.
* **Returning Adults are a product fit problem.** Their prep mix is reasonable,
  they show up, and they credential worst. G1: they are older adults re entering
  education after a long gap while holding jobs and families. The barriers are
  scheduling, format and retake cost. G2 slide 10 says it in four words:
  they show up, they prep, the product fails them.
* **GED Ready Completers are the target state,** and even they collapse in
  specific places, which is the bridge into the geography section.

**The geography finding, and it is a structural one.** In three named urban
boroughs, credential rates are low across **all four segments**, including the
best prepared one (G1 part 3, G2 slide 11). Candidates doing everything right
still do not credential there. The team's conclusion is that this is not a prep
behaviour problem at all: it is testing site density against candidate volume,
and no behavioural campaign will move it until access is fixed. Meanwhile a
different state's counties lead the top rankings across three of the four
segments simultaneously, which the team flagged as a model to study rather than
a result to celebrate, recommending a qualitative case study to find out what is
working there before spending anywhere else.

**Every county, borough and state name paired with a rate or a gap: withheld.**
G1 part 3 and part 4, G2 slides 11 and 12.

**The recommendation, which is the part that shows consulting judgement.** One
playbook, four levers, sequenced by dependency rather than by size (G2 slides 13
and 14): structural access audits first, because behavioural campaigns cannot
work where the barrier is physical; then convert the digital self starters,
which needs no structural prerequisite; then embed GED Ready inside Adult Ed
programmes with device and internet access; then redesign the format for
returning adults. And commission the study of the high performing state **now**,
before committing money to the priority geographies, so there is a playbook to
copy rather than a guess to fund.

Sequencing recommendations by what blocks what, and explicitly telling the
client not to run a campaign in the place with the worst numbers, is a better
case study beat than any of the model output.

**The assumptions are declared, not buried.** G2 appendix A lists eight,
including the zero engagement exclusion, the minimum county size, the mobile app
drop, the post hoc nature of the cluster names, and the association not causation
line. One entry excludes a county from its own ranking as a likely data artifact
and says why. That appendix is evidence of honest method and it belongs in the
case study.
